    <h2 data-section id="bennu-intro" tabIndex={-1}>A grain older than the Sun</h2>
    <p>Most of Bennu’s returned material formed within the young Solar System or changed later inside its parent asteroid. But a small number of mineral grains began their lives earlier, in the outflows of stars that existed before the Sun. They are called {k("presolar grains","Mineral grains that condensed around stars before the Solar System formed; isotope ratios can preserve clues to stellar nucleosynthesis.")} because their atoms condensed into solids before the cloud of gas and dust that became our planetary system. In a grain only hundreds of nanometres across, isotope ratios can preserve a chemical fingerprint that ordinary Solar System processes would not produce. {cite("cite-1a","1")}</p>
    <p>That is the unusual value of the Bennu measurements reported by Ann Nguyen and colleagues. The team looked for carbon-rich silicon carbide and graphite, alongside oxygen-rich silicate and oxide grains, in polished sections of three returned particles. These are rare survivors: the original asteroid was assembled from material that experienced impacts, heating, and liquid-water alteration, processes capable of destroying or transforming primitive minerals.</p>
    <h2 data-section id="bennu-rocks" tabIndex={-1}>Two kinds of rock, different histories</h2>
    <p>The particles came from two textural settings. Two were described as angular; their fine-grained matrices are relatively continuous and contain common secondary minerals formed during aqueous alteration. The third, OREX-803172-0, is hummocky—a rough, fragment-rich breccia. Its rounded clasts have boundaries against the surrounding matrix, recording pieces of rock that were mixed together after forming different mineral textures.</p>
    <p>In the paper’s Figure 1, a backscattered-electron image is paired with maps of oxygen, silicon, magnesium, iron and sulfur. The maps make the clast boundaries visible in composition: the yellow outlines identify sulfur-rich regions; other outlined clasts differ in silicon or resemble the surrounding matrix. Three sulfur-rich clasts, each tens to hundreds of micrometres across, are particularly important because many oxygen-rich presolar grains were found within them. The image documents rock texture and elemental distribution; it does not itself identify each stardust grain.</p>
    <p>That distinction matters. The maps show where elements occur. Isotopic analysis is needed to identify material whose oxygen or carbon ratios fall outside the range expected for ordinary Solar System minerals. The authors combine those lines of evidence, rather than treating an unusual-looking spot as proof of stellar origin.</p>
    <h2 data-section id="bennu-isotopes" tabIndex={-1}>Reading a stellar fingerprint</h2>
    <p>Stars manufacture elements through nuclear reactions, and different stellar environments leave different isotope proportions in their ejecta. When a mineral grain forms around a star and survives its journey into the interstellar medium, its isotope ratios may remain anomalous relative to the Solar System average. Researchers can therefore classify some presolar grains by comparing measured isotope compositions with stellar models and with previously studied meteorite grains.</p>
    <p>Nguyen’s group used electron microscopy and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy to characterize the samples, then nanoscale secondary-ion mass spectrometry to measure isotope ratios in targeted regions. Across the three particles, they report 63 carbon-rich grains and 25 oxygen-rich grains with isotopic compositions consistent with presolar material. Because the survey examined only selected sections and only three particles, those counts describe the measured material, not every grain in Bennu’s returned collection. {cite("cite-1b","1")}</p>
    <p>For the carbon-rich silicon-carbide population, the estimated abundance in Bennu was 55 parts per million, with asymmetric uncertainty of +12 and −10 ppm. The graphite estimate was about 1 ppm, with a comparatively large uncertainty. Oxygen-rich presolar grains averaged roughly 4 ppm across the angular particles, while specific clasts in the hummocky particle yielded higher local values. These numbers are measurements inferred from grain counts, sampled area and analytical detection; they are not a bulk chemical assay of all Bennu material.</p>
    <aside className="concept concept-inline" aria-label="Key concept related to Reading a stellar fingerprint"><strong>Key concept · Isotope ratio</strong>An isotope is a form of an element with a different number of neutrons. The ratio between isotopes can preserve a record of the nuclear environment where a mineral formed, even after the grain is mixed into asteroid material.</aside>
    <h2 data-section id="bennu-alteration" tabIndex={-1}>Water left a patchwork</h2>
    <p>The contrast between clasts is evidence that Bennu’s parent body was not altered uniformly. Around 72% of the oxygen-rich presolar grains in the study were located within three sulfur-rich clasts in the hummocky particle. The authors infer that these clasts experienced less aqueous alteration than the surrounding materials, allowing more fragile presolar silicates to survive. This is a local preservation signal, not proof that the entire hummocky rock escaped water.</p>
    <p>That inference sits alongside broad evidence that Bennu’s parent body interacted with liquid water. Many minerals in the returned samples are secondary phases, and spacecraft observations identified hydrated material and carbonate veins on the asteroid. Water can dissolve or transform some original grains; its effects depend on temperature, chemistry, duration and the pathways available to fluids. A clast sheltered from one alteration episode may preserve minerals that are scarce elsewhere.</p>
    <p>The result adds a fine-scale map to the asteroid’s history: not simply “wet” or “dry,” but pockets with different mineral histories within material that was later assembled and reworked. The grain distribution therefore helps reconstruct both the inventory inherited from before the Solar System and the later processes that modified it.</p>
    <h2 data-section id="bennu-supernova" tabIndex={-1}>A striking proportion, carefully stated</h2>
    <p>The paper’s headline result concerns the inferred stellar sources of presolar silicon-carbide grains. About 31% of the Bennu SiC grains identified in this work were classified as having a supernova origin, compared with about 5% in the comparison population of other chondritic samples discussed by the authors. That is approximately a sixfold difference in the proportion of this particular grain type assigned to a stellar source. It does not mean Bennu contains six times more total dust, six times more asteroid material, or six times more presolar grains overall. {cite("cite-1c","1")}</p>
    <p>Why might Bennu be enriched in this subset? The authors discuss the possibility that its parent body incorporated material from a nearby supernova contribution, which could have changed the mix of presolar grains available during Solar System formation. The measured isotope signatures support the classification of individual grains; the population-level comparison motivates a possible source-enrichment explanation. Neither observation establishes a single nearby explosion as the unique cause. More samples and broader comparisons are needed to distinguish source mixing, sampling effects and preservation differences.</p>
    <h2 data-section id="bennu-limits" tabIndex={-1}>What the grains cannot tell us alone</h2>
    <p>The study’s strength is the combination of petrography, elemental mapping and nanoscale isotope measurements. Its limits are just as important: only three particles were analysed, the targets are microscopic and rare, and the inferred stellar classifications depend on reference fields and astrophysical models. The observed clast context strengthens an interpretation about preservation, but does not provide a complete chronology of every water episode on Bennu’s parent asteroid.</p>
    <p>The paper also received an Author Correction on 18 February 2026. It changes the Fig. 2b legend: an X-grain symbol should be a filled grey triangle, not an unfilled grey circle. The correction does not alter the Fig. 1 maps used here, but it matters for any future reproduction or discussion of Fig. 2b. This page therefore links the correction as a separate bibliographic record. {cite("cite-2a","2","ref-2")}</p>
    <p>Bennu’s grains are not a direct recording of one stellar event. They are samples of a mixed and altered asteroid whose rare mineral fragments preserve partial histories. Their value lies in that combination: evidence from before the Sun, carried through later geological change, with uncertainty visible in the way the evidence is collected and interpreted.</p>
